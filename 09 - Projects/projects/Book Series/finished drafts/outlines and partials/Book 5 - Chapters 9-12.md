# Chapter Nine: The Homing Beacon

The wind coming off the Greenvale Reach carried the smell of sawdust and fresh-cut pine. I stood at the treeline where the forest thinned into open grassland, and beyond that grassland, the first structures of the Unity Market rose like bones skinned of their flesh. Half-built stalls. Scaffolding climbed by human hands and hybrid claws alike. Banners of deep indigo and silver hung from crossbeams, their edges snapping in the wind like the tongues of something trying to speak.

I counted the workers. Forty-seven humans. Twelve hybrids. Three Vaelthornian overseers standing on an elevated platform with their arms folded, watching. Cataloguing. The Vaelthornian insignia — the seven-pointed star of the Shadow Mirror — burned into the leather of their coats.

They were building a marketplace. A place where humans and hybrids would trade goods, share space, break bread together like some kind of painted parable about unity. It was the crown jewel of the Accord, the proof that the old wars were over, that the new world had arrived.

It was a beautiful, lying insult.

I pulled my cloak tighter against the cold and descended the ridge in a low crouch, staying below the sightline. The Auditor's voice hadn't spoken since the border crossing, but the weight of its last instruction sat heavy in my chest. Become the anchor. Embed the shard. Let the resonance find its shape.

I'd been designated Apex Scout three days prior. The classification had arrived without ceremony — a notation burned into the leather of my forearm by the Auditor's Discord sigil, violet-black ink that pulsed when I flexed my wrist. Apex Scout. Primary anchor for harvest operations in the Aethorian theater. It meant I was the first point of contact, the lodestone that would draw the others in when the time came.

It meant I was the one who would betray them all.

The shard sat in a leather pouch against my hip. Oakhaven Rift shard — a fragment of crystallized Discord harvested from the ruins of the Oakhaven outpost after the purge. It hummed against my thigh, a low vibration that matched the cadence of my breathing. The Auditor had calibrated it specifically for this deployment. One shard, one location, one permanent high-resonance hole that would serve as a beacon for everything that followed.

I reached the outer perimeter of the construction site as the sun dipped toward the western mountains. The workers were closing up for the evening, hauling tools to wagons, dousing lanterns. The light went golden, then amber, then the deep bruised purple of a day surrendering itself to night.

I found a position in the crawl space beneath a half-finished grain exchange. The foundation beams were exposed — rough-hewn oak and iron bracing driven deep into the packed earth. I lay on my stomach in the dirt and assessed the structural layout with the mechanical detachment the Auditor had drilled into me. Load-bearing points. Stress intersections. The places where the bones of the building met the bones of the ground.

The market's foundation was a network. Beams radiating outward from a central hub like the spokes of a wheel, each one anchored in concrete footings that had been poured three weeks prior. The concrete was still curing. Still soft enough in places to accept an intrusion.

I chose a junction point two feet below the surface where three beams converged. The soil here was clay-heavy, packed tight by rain and the weight of construction above. I drew a thin blade from my boot and carved a narrow channel in the earth, then pressed the shard into the depression I'd created.

The shard bit into my palm as I activated it. Not pain, exactly. More like the sensation of something cold pressing inward against the bone, finding the spaces between the joints, settling into the architecture of the hand as though it had always lived there. The Discord hummed — a frequency below hearing but felt in the teeth, the sinuses, the base of the skull.

I pushed the shard into the foundation junction and sealed the earth over it with careful, practiced movements. Packed the clay. Smoothed the surface. Brushed away the evidence with the back of my hand.

When I pulled my hand free, the ground shuddered. Just once. A single tremor that passed through the foundation beams and into the structures above, so subtle that no human or hybrid above would have noticed. But I felt it. The shard had found its home. It had begun to sing.

I lay there in the dirt beneath the grain exchange, breathing in the smell of wet clay and new wood, and I listened to the beacon wake.

It was a sound like a throat being cleared at the edge of a lullaby. Low. Insistent. The beginning of a melody that would grow louder over the coming days, humming through the ground, through the beams, through the bones of every structure built on top of it. The resonance would spread outward in invisible threads, weaving itself into the foundations of the market, into the roots of the land beneath the market, into the water table, the stone, the air.

By the time the Unity Market opened its doors, the beacon would be woven into everything. And the Shadow Mirror would know exactly where to look.

I pulled myself out of the crawl space as full dark settled. The construction site was empty now, lanterns extinguished, wagons gone. Only the skeleton of the market remained, its half-built bones reaching toward a sky thick with stars.

I climbed to the roof of the grain exchange and lay flat against the thatch, staring up. The stars here were different from the ones over Vaelthorne. Brighter. Closer. As though the sky itself was leaning down to listen.

The shard hummed beneath me, through the beams, through the thatch, through the meat of my back and into the architecture of my spine. I could feel it mapping the space around me — every beam, every joint, every nail driven into fresh wood. The beacon was already working, already sending its signal outward into the dark, a pulse of Discord tuned to a frequency that only the Mirror's instruments could read.

I closed my eyes and thought about what it meant.

The Unity Market. Humans and hybrids working side by side, building something together. I'd watched them all afternoon — a human woman handing a hammer to a wolf-kin laborer, their fingers brushing in the exchange. A group of adolescent hybrids laughing at a joke told by a human teenager. An old man, human, leaning on his shovel while a raven-kin woman showed him a trick for sharpening a blade.

It was, by every measurable standard, beautiful.

And it was built on a lie.

The Shadow Mirror didn't want unity. The Mirror wanted harvest. The hybrids here — the wolf-kin, the raven-kin, the bear-kin, the dozens of others who'd come to Aethoria under the banner of the Accord — they were filters. That was what the Auditor had taught me. Not weapons. Not soldiers. Filters. Living organisms whose hybrid physiology could absorb and process Discord in ways that pure humans could not. The Shadow Mirror had been using hybrids for centuries, not as allies or partners, but as infrastructure. As tools. As filters for a force that would otherwise consume them.

And the market — this beautiful, impossible market where humans and hybrids built something together — was the perfect cover. A place where hybrids gathered in concentration. A place where the Discord could be harvested efficiently, quietly, from dozens of subjects simultaneously.

I had planted a beacon in its foundations. In a month, when the Mirror's harvesters came, they would follow the signal to this place. And every hybrid who had set foot in this market, every hybrid who had breathed its air or touched its beams or traded in its stalls, would be marked. Catalogued. Filtered.

I pressed my palms against the thatch and felt the shard's pulse echo back through the building, through my hands, into my chest.

I was seventeen years old, and I had just become the reason the world would end.

---

The morning brought rain. A soft, persistent drizzle that turned the construction site into a landscape of mud and dripping thatch. I watched from a copse of birch trees two hundred yards north of the market perimeter, eating cold rations and mapping the guard rotations.

Three Vaelthornian overseers. Two shifts. Twelve hours each. They patrolled in overlapping circuits, never out of sight of each other, never leaving the construction site unattended. Professional. Predictable. The kind of security detail that assumed the threats would come from outside.

They weren't wrong about that.

I catalogued the patrol patterns in my head, committing the timing to memory. The gap between rotations was ninety seconds — the window when one patrol reached the far end of the circuit and the other was still moving into position. Ninety seconds of reduced visibility. Enough for a trained operative to cross the perimeter, complete an objective, and withdraw without detection.

But I wasn't here to infiltrate the perimeter. I'd already done that. The shard was embedded, the beacon was singing, and no amount of patrolling would undo what had been planted in the foundations.

I was here to watch.

The workers arrived at dawn. Humans first, in clusters of three and four, carrying tool bundles and lunch pails. Then the hybrids, moving alone or in pairs, their various anatomies leaving distinct tracks in the mud. Wolf-kin prints, heavy and wide. Raven-kin prints, thin and sharp. The earth bore the record of their coming like a ledger, each entry clear and legible in the wet clay.

I watched a human man and a fox-kin woman carry a stack of lumber together. Their rhythm was synchronized — years of working alongside each other, perhaps. Or perhaps it was simpler than that. Perhaps it was the natural coordination of two bodies that had learned to move as one, human and hybrid fitting together like pieces of a mechanism that had been designed to function in tandem.

It was, I thought again, beautiful.

It was also a trap.

The fox-kin woman set down her end of the lumber and wiped her hands on her apron. She turned toward the treeline — toward me — and for one sharp instant, I thought she'd seen me. My hand went to the blade at my belt. My breath stopped.

But she wasn't looking at me. She was looking at the sky, at the rain clouds parting to reveal a strip of pale blue, and she smiled. A simple, unguarded smile. The kind of expression that had nothing to do with war or Discord or the Shadow Mirror or any of the machinery of power that ground beneath the surface of her daily life.

She smiled because the rain was stopping. Because the sky was clearing. Because sometimes the world offered a small, uncomplicated kindness, and the right response was to receive it.

I lowered my hand from my blade. I sat in the mud and the birch leaves and I watched her smile, and something inside me cracked — not broke, not shattered, but cracked, the way ice cracks on a river in early spring. A fissure running through the surface, revealing the dark water moving beneath.

I had planted a beacon in the foundations of her world. I had marked her, and everyone like her, for harvest. And she was smiling at the sky.

The rain stopped. The workers returned to their tasks. I remained in the copse, cataloguing, watching, waiting. The shard beneath the grain exchange sang its low, patient song, and the morning light moved across the construction site like a hand smoothing a bedsheet, revealing the shapes of things hidden beneath.

---

By midday, the market was taking shape. The central hub — a circular clearing where the main thoroughfares converged — had been paved with flat stones carried up from the Greenvale riverbed. The stones were pale granite, flecked with mica that caught the light and scattered it in tiny sparks. Workers were fitting them together like a mosaic, tapping them into place with rubber mallets, leveling the surface with meticulous care.

I moved to a new observation point — the upper floor of a half-finished tannery on the market's eastern edge. The building was still open to the air, its walls only three courses high, but the second floor joists were in place and the roof beams provided cover. I crouched between two support posts and watched the paving crew through a gap in the wall.

The central hub was directly above the shard.

I could feel it — the resonance rising through the packed earth, through the granite foundation of the hub, into the stones being laid by the workers. The beacon's signal was stronger now, more defined, its hum settling into a steady pulse that matched the rhythm of hammer strikes and worker conversation. It was blending in. Becoming part of the fabric of the place. Within days it would be indistinguishable from the ambient noise of a functioning market — a background hum that no one noticed because it had always been there.

That was the design. The Auditor had been specific about this. A beacon that announced itself would be discovered and destroyed. A beacon that became part of the architecture would endure.

I pulled the shard's resonance signature into focus, feeling it through the beams beneath me. The signal was clean — a single, unwavering tone that carried Discord in its harmonic structure like water carried silt. Pure enough to be detected by Mirror instruments at a distance of fifty miles. Strong enough to cut through the natural interference of a populated area. Tuned precisely to the frequency that the Shadow Mirror's harvesters would recognize.

It was, by every technical standard, perfect.

It was also the death warrant for every hybrid in Aethoria.

I forced my breathing to slow. In through the nose, out through the mouth. The Auditor's breathing exercise, designed to suppress the sympathetic nervous response that might compromise operational clarity. I wasn't supposed to feel anything about this. The classification was Apex Scout, not Apex Conscience. The work was observation and placement, not judgment.

But the fox-kin woman's smile stayed behind my eyes like a brand.

A commotion below drew my attention. Two workers — a human man and a bear-kin youth, no older than myself — had gotten into an argument over the placement of a cornerstone. The human wanted it shifted six inches to the left. The bear-kin wanted it centered on the beam junction. Their voices carried upward, sharp with frustration, then softer as a third worker — an older human woman — intervened.

"That's enough, both of you," she said, her voice carrying the easy authority of someone who had spent decades managing people. "Center it on the beam. The beam doesn't lie."

The bear-kin youth looked at the human man. The human man looked at the beam. Then at the stone. Then at the youth.

"Fine," the man said. "Center it."

They lifted the stone together, grunting with the weight, and set it down on the beam junction. The woman checked the level, nodded, and moved on to the next task. The two workers stood for a moment, catching their breath, and then the bear-kin extended a hand. The human took it. They shook once, firmly, and returned to work.

I watched the handshake from my position in the rafters. Watched the casual trust in it, the unthinking ease of two people who had decided that their collaboration mattered more than their disagreement. It was, in miniature, everything the Unity Market was supposed to represent.

And I had planted a bomb in its foundation.

Not a bomb. A beacon. The distinction mattered, or it didn't. The result was the same.

I pressed my forehead against the rough wood of the roof beam and closed my eyes. The shard's pulse throbbed through the timber, through my skull, into the soft tissue of my brain. I could feel the beacon's reach expanding with every passing hour, its threads of resonance weaving outward through the soil, the stone, the air. In a week it would encompass the entire market. In two weeks, the surrounding neighborhoods. In a month, the whole of Aethoria's eastern district.

And then the Mirror would come.

I opened my eyes and looked down at the market — at the workers, the stalls, the banners, the stones being fitted together with such care — and I allowed myself one moment of something that might have been grief.

Then I closed the moment, tucked it into the dark place behind my ribs where I kept the things I could not afford to feel, and returned to my observation.

The sun tracked across the sky. The workers took a midday break, sitting in clusters on the half-finished walls and scaffolding, sharing food and conversation. I ate my rations — cold salt pork and hardtack, the same food I'd eaten every day since crossing the border — and watched the social patterns emerge. Humans sat with humans. Hybrids sat with hybrids. But there were outliers — clusters where the groups mixed, where laughter crossed the species line, where a raven-kin girl shared an apple with a human boy and neither of them thought it unusual.

The outliers were the ones who would be hurt most. The ones who had believed the lie most completely. The ones who had opened themselves to a connection that the Shadow Mirror would exploit.

I chewed the salt pork and tasted nothing.

---

Evening fell. The workers departed in the same order they'd arrived — humans first, then hybrids, then the Vaelthornian overseers, who conducted a final patrol of the site before extinguishing the perimeter lanterns. I remained in the tannery, waiting for full dark, listening to the shard's pulse grow steadier in the silence.

When the last lantern died, I descended from the tannery and crossed to the central hub. The granite paving stones were cool beneath my boots, their surfaces still damp from the morning rain. I knelt at the center of the hub and placed my palm flat against the stone.

The resonance was strong here. Stronger than it should have been after only a single day. I pressed harder, feeling the vibration travel up through my arm, into my shoulder, down my spine. The beacon was not just humming — it was growing. Feeding on the Discord that naturally accumulated in a place where so many hybrids gathered. Each hybrid body in the market was a filter, and each filter left traces — residual Discord that settled into the environment like sediment into water. The beacon was absorbing that sediment, incorporating it, using it to amplify its own signal.

I pulled my hand away. The stone was warm where I'd touched it.

I stood and turned a slow circle, taking in the market in its nighttime configuration. The stalls were empty, their frames dark against the starlit sky. The banners hung limp in the windless air. The scaffolding cast angular shadows across the packed earth. It was a skeleton of a place, waiting to be filled with the noise and color and chaos of commerce.

In three weeks, it would open. Humans and hybrids would pour through its gates, buying and selling and laughing and touching, building the web of connections that would make the Unity Market the beating heart of the Accord. And beneath their feet, in the foundations and the stones and the packed clay, the beacon would sing its patient, deadly song.

I climbed to the roof of the grain exchange and lay on my back. The stars were out — the same bright, close stars I'd watched the night before. The sky was clear and cold and enormous, pressing down on the world like a hand on a wound.

The shard hummed through the thatch, through the beams, through my body. Its pulse had settled into a rhythm that matched my heartbeat, as though it had adopted me as its metronome. As though the beacon and I were parts of the same mechanism, each dependent on the other for function.

I thought about the Auditor. About the lessons in Discord calibration, the hours of breathing exercises, the clinical precision with which it had explained the purpose of hybrids in the Mirror's architecture. Filters, it had said. Not weapons. Not soldiers. Filters. Their physiology absorbs Discord that would otherwise destabilize human settlements. They are living infrastructure.

Living infrastructure. The phrase had settled into me like a stone into water, sinking through layers of training and discipline and arriving, finally, at the soft place at my core where the things I didn't allow myself to think still lived.

I was hybrid. The Auditor knew this. The Shadow Mirror knew this. I was a filter, just like the wolf-kin and the raven-kin and the bear-kin who had built the market with their hands and their trust. The difference was that I had been designated Apex Scout — a filter that had been repurposed, turned from passive infrastructure into active instrument. A filter that could think, and plan, and betray.

I pressed my palms against my eyes until I saw stars that had nothing to do with the sky.

The Beacon sang beneath me. The moon rose over the Greenvale Reach, pale and swollen, casting the half-built market in a light that made it look like a ruin — like something that had already fallen and was being mourned by the earth that held its bones.

I lay on the roof and listened to the lullaby that was not a lullaby, the song that was not a song, and I let the sound fill the spaces inside me where the grief lived. The singing was the thing. The singing was always the thing. Not the melody, not the words, but the act of making sound in the darkness, of pressing vibration against the silence and demanding that it yield.

The beacon sang. The moon watched. And somewhere in the distance, beyond the mountains, beyond the borders, beyond the reach of the light, the Shadow Mirror turned its seven faces toward the sound and began to listen.

The singing was the thing. The singing was the thing. The singing was the thing.

And the thing was almost finished.

# Chapter Ten: The Mirror

Midnight came to Aethoria like a held breath finally released.

I lay on the tavern roof with my cheek pressed against cold slate, watching the city below through eyes that had learned to see in the dark. The Midnight Court convened three times per year — at the solstice, at the equinox, and at the turning of the administrative calendar. Tonight was the third. The governing body of Aethoria's human district had gathered in the great hall of the Consulate, and the streets around the building were thick with torches, guards, and the low murmur of citizens who had come to watch.

I wasn't here for the Court. I was here for the patrol.

He appeared at the end of Copper Lane at precisely twelve minutes past midnight, moving with the same stride I'd known since childhood — long, purposeful, each step placed with the unconscious confidence of someone who believed he belonged exactly where he was. Lukas. My brother. Royal Guard Captain of Aethoria, resplendent in the midnight-blue armor of the King's Own, his sword at his hip and his cloak catching the torchlight like a wing.

He looked like a hero. He looked like every story I'd ever been told about what a person could be.

He looked like he was happy.

I watched him round the corner onto the Consulate's approach, his patrol route taking him past the gathered citizens, past the torches, past the guards posted at the main gate. A woman in the crowd — human, middle-aged, wrapped in a shawl — reached out and touched his arm as he passed. He turned, smiled, said something I couldn't hear. She laughed. He moved on.

He is happy, the voice said.

I didn't flinch. The Auditor's voice had been arriving in fragments since the border crossing — short, clinical observations delivered without preamble or explanation, as though my mind were a ledger and it was simply updating the entries.

He is happy because you are the one who was discarded.

I closed my eyes. The slate was cold against my cheek. The voice was right, and we both knew it. Lukas had been the son who stayed, the son who excelled, the son who earned his place in the light through talent and determination and the kind of uncomplicated moral conviction that I had never been able to sustain. I was the son who had been taken. The son who had been broken down and rebuilt into something that served the Shadow Mirror's purpose. The son who had been discarded — not by Lukas, but by the architecture of power that had decided one brother was worth keeping and the other was worth consuming.

Lukas didn't know this. He didn't know about the Shadow Mirror's true design, about the Auditor, about the Discord sigil burned into my forearm or the shard pulsing in the foundations of the Unity Market three miles to the east. He knew that I had been taken, that I had been trained, that I had become something he didn't understand. He knew enough to grieve.

He didn't know enough to stop me.

I opened my eyes and watched him reach the far end of his patrol route. He paused at the intersection of Copper Lane and the High Bridge, turning to survey the street behind him. The torchlight caught the edge of his jaw, the line of his throat, the silver insignia at his collar. He was twenty years old. He had the build of a swordsman — lean, precise, every muscle calibrated for efficiency. He had our mother's eyes and our father's hands and the kind of steady gaze that made people feel safe in his presence.

I had none of these things. I had the Auditor's training and the Mirror's sigil and a body that had been honed into a weapon of such specific lethality that it could no longer distinguish between its targets and its kin.

Poor Lukas, the voice observed. Still reaching for the hand that let you go.

I sat up. The movement was smooth, practiced, the kind of motion that produced no sound and no silhouette against the night sky. I stood on the tavern roof, the wind pulling at my cloak, and I looked down at my brother standing at the end of Copper Lane.

I was done watching. The observation was complete. The shard was embedded, the beacon was singing, the market's foundations were saturated with the Mirror's resonance. There was nothing more to see from this rooftop, nothing more to learn from this angle. The next phase required proximity.

I stepped to the edge of the roof and dropped.

The landing was quiet — the Auditor's training made sure of that. My boots touched the alley's cobblestones without sound, the impact absorbed by bent knees and a controlled exhalation. I straightened, checked the alley's length in both directions, and moved.

The streets of Aethoria at midnight were a different country than the streets of Aethoria by day. The noise was gone, the commerce, the crowds. What remained was the city's skeleton — the stone buildings, the narrow lanes, the torchlight playing across wet surfaces like fingers across a drumskin. I moved through it all with the Auditor's efficiency, keeping to the shadows, adjusting my pace to match the rhythm of Lukas's patrol.

He was heading east, toward the market district. Toward the Unity Market. Toward the beacon.

I didn't need to follow him. The beacon would do its work regardless of who walked above it. But the Auditor had been specific about this phase: observe the primary subject in proximity to the anchor point. Document behavioral patterns. Note any anomalies in hybrid-Discord interaction.

I was, in the Mirror's taxonomy, a research instrument. My brother was the primary subject. And the beacon beneath the Unity Market was the controlled environment in which the experiment would unfold.

I rounded a corner and saw him again — a hundred yards ahead, his silhouette moving through the torchlight with the steady rhythm of a professional on patrol. He was walking alone. His backup was three streets to the south, running the parallel route. For this stretch, it was just Lukas and the empty street and the night.

I closed the distance. Not quickly — the Auditor's training made speed a secondary priority to silence — but steadily, each step placed with the deliberation of a chess piece moving across a board. Fifty yards. Forty. Thirty. I could see the details of his armor now, the scratches on the leather vambraces, the dent in his left pauldron from a training bout gone wrong. The small, human imperfections that made him real.

Twenty yards. He stopped.

I froze.

He stood at the center of the intersection, his hand resting on his sword hilt, his head tilted slightly to one side. Listening. I held my breath and pressed myself against the wall of a building, letting the shadow of an awning cover me completely.

He stood there for eleven seconds. I counted.

Then he relaxed, shook his head as though dismissing a thought, and continued walking. His pace was slightly faster now, the stride of someone who wanted to finish the route and return to warmth.

I let him go. I watched his silhouette shrink and blur in the distance, watched the torchlight swallow him, watched the night reclaim the space where he had been.

He was heading toward the Unity Market. He would walk over the beacon. He would pass above the shard and feel nothing, because the shard's resonance operated on a frequency that human senses couldn't perceive. He would walk over the spot where I had planted the instrument of his world's destruction, and he would feel nothing at all.

I stood in the empty intersection and pressed my back against the cold stone wall and breathed.

The operation proceeds on schedule, the voice observed. The primary subject has been documented in proximity to the anchor point. No behavioral anomalies detected.

"I know," I said aloud, to no one.

You are not required to respond to internal observations. The protocol is passive.

"I know."

You are experiencing a sympathetic response. It is noted and classified. Continue the patrol.

I pushed off the wall and moved into the night.

---

I reached the Unity Market an hour before dawn. The construction site was dark and still, the half-built structures standing like sentinels against a sky that was just beginning to lighten in the east. The shard's pulse was stronger now — I could feel it through the ground from fifty yards out, a steady, insistent hum that resonated in the fillings of my teeth.

I crossed the perimeter without effort. The overnight guard was a single Vaelthornian, posted at the main gate, who had fallen asleep with his chin on his chest. I passed him like a shadow, crossed the central hub, and knelt at the exact spot where I had embedded the shard.

The earth was warm. Not the residual warmth of a sun-baked surface — this was deeper, coming from below, radiating upward through the clay like heat from a body. The shard was active, growing, its Discord signature spreading through the foundation like roots through soil. I pressed my palm flat against the stone and felt the beacon's full force for the first time.

It was a roar. A constant, multilayered roar of Discord energy, compressed and channeled by the shard's crystalline structure into a signal that would cut through the ambient noise of a living city like a blade through cloth. The beacon was broadcasting on every frequency that the Mirror's instruments could detect, sending its call outward in invisible waves that would reach the farthest corners of Vaelthorne within days.

I pulled my hand away. My palm was tingling, the skin flushed pink where it had touched the stone.

I stood and backed away from the hub, moving with care. The beacon was now the most dangerous object in Aethoria — not because of its destructive potential, but because of its informational value. Anyone with the right instruments could detect it. Anyone with the right training could trace it to its source. And anyone with the right allegiance could report it to the Shadow Mirror.

Which was, of course, the point.

I climbed to the top of the scaffolding on the market's northern edge and looked out over the construction site. The sun was rising now, painting the eastern sky in shades of gold and rose. The first workers would arrive within the hour. They would come with their tools and their lunch pails and their easy conversations, and they would walk over the beacon without knowing it, and they would build the rest of the market on top of the trap I had laid for them.

I thought about the fox-kin woman. I thought about the bear-kin youth. I thought about the old man with the shovel and the raven-kin woman with the sharpening trick. I thought about every hand that had touched these stones, every voice that had echoed in this space, every laugh that had crossed the species line and made the world feel, for a moment, like the place it was supposed to be.

Then I turned my back on the market and descended the scaffolding and walked east, toward the border, toward the next phase of the operation.

The sun climbed behind me. The beacon hummed beneath the rising market. And somewhere in the city behind me, my brother was waking in his quarters, pulling on his armor, preparing for another day of believing that the world was the thing he thought it was.

The moon had set. The dawn was breaking. And the singing — the singing that was always the thing — had become something new. Not a lullaby now. Not a comfort. A summons. A call that would draw the Shadow Mirror's instruments across the border, through the forests, into the heart of Aethoria, and the sound it made as it arrived would be the sound of every beautiful lie dissolving into the truth it had always contained.

The singing was the thing. The singing was the thing. The singing was the thing.

And the thing was done.

---

I returned to my safe house in the river district as the morning crowd filled the streets. The safe house was a rented room above a chandler's shop, its window overlooking the Greenvale where the river curved south toward the border. I locked the door behind me, drew the curtains, and sat on the floor with my back against the wall.

The Auditor's voice was waiting.

The deployment is complete. The beacon is operational. You are reclassified from Apex Scout to Active Observer. Maintain position in Aethoria theater. Monitor beacon activation. Report any anomalies in Discord harvest patterns.

I said nothing. The room was small, dark, smelling of tallow and woodsmoke. The kind of room that a traveling merchant might rent for a week before moving on, leaving nothing behind but a dent in the mattress and a faint impression in the pillow.

You are experiencing elevated cortisol. The sympathetic nervous response is within acceptable parameters but trending toward chronic activation. Recommend breathing exercises.

"I'm fine."

You are not fine. Fine is not a classification. The protocol does not recognize fine.

I pressed my palms against my eyes and breathed. In through the nose, out through the mouth. The Auditor's exercise. The one that was supposed to suppress the emotional response and restore operational clarity.

It didn't work. It hadn't worked in days. Every time I closed my eyes, I saw the fox-kin woman smiling at the sky. Every time I breathed, I tasted the wet clay beneath the grain exchange. Every time I lay still, I felt the shard's pulse through the beams, through the earth, through the architecture of the world I was helping to dismantle.

I was a weapon. I had been trained as a weapon. The Auditor had been specific about this — my body was an instrument of the Shadow Mirror's will, calibrated for a specific function, deployed for a specific purpose. Weapons did not feel. Weapons did not question. Weapons performed their function and were retired when their utility was exhausted.
