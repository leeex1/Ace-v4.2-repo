# Book 5: Shadows That Speak

## Chapter 17: The Return to Vaelthorne

The road back to Vaelthorne tasted like iron and ash.

I rode hard through the Greywood, pushing the black mare past what any reasonable handler would allow. She was borrowed from a stable outside Millhaven -- a sturdy thing, not pretty, but fast enough to outrun the questions clawing at the back of my skull. The trees blurred past in smears of grey and green, their branches reaching like skeletal fingers trying to snag me from the saddle. I ducked beneath them without thinking. Muscle memory. The same instinct that had carried me through the throne room in Aethoria, through Lukas's blade, through the impossible light of the Hero's awakening.

The wound in my side still bled. Not freely -- I'd packed it with wolfroot and bound it tight -- but enough that every jolt of the horse sent a hot lance through my ribs. I ignored it. Pain was a language I spoke fluently.

"You're quiet," I said to the Voice.

Nothing. It had been nothing for three days now. Since the throne room. Since the light had torn through me like a blade made of sunrise, and the Voice -- the constant, certain, guiding Voice of the Auditor -- had gone silent. Not gone. I could feel it still, coiled at the base of my skull like a serpent sleeping. But the absolute certainty that had governed every kill, every mission, every breath of my service to Vaelthorne had developed a hairline crack.

And through that crack, something else was leaking in.

I saw Lukas's face every time I closed my eyes. Not the face I'd fought -- jaw set, eyes burning with that idiot stubbornness, sword raised against his own blood. The other face. The one from when we were children, lying in the wheat fields outside Oakhaven, watching clouds drift like slow ships across the summer sky. The face that said the world was wide enough for both of us.

That face was worse than the sword.

The Greywood thinned. The road widened. And there, rising from the haze like a bruise against the sky, was Vaelthorne.

I'd always hated how it looked from a distance. Aethoria had its crystalline spires and its golden walls, all light and aspiration. Vaelthorne was built from black stone quarried in the deep places of the earth, and it wore that origin openly. The towers were jagged, uneven, like teeth in a mouth that hadn't learned to smile. The walls were thick, functional, unornamented. There were no gardens visible from outside, no flowing water, no color. Just stone and shadow and the faint blue glow of the rift-wells that powered everything.

Home. Such as it was.

I slowed the mare to a walk as I approached the outer gate. Two sentries in dark armor -- Vaelthorne standard, no insignia, because we didn't advertise -- looked me over without expression. One of them recognized me. I saw it in the way his posture shifted, the way his hand tightened on his spear.

"Shadowfang," he said.

"Open the gate."

He did. They always did. There were only three people in Vaelthorne who could command that kind of immediate compliance: Lord Vaelthorne himself, Kaelen, and me. The first two earned it through decades of power. I'd earned it through a reputation built on corpses.

The city swallowed me the way it always did -- cold, dark, efficient. The streets were clean but not pleasant. Vaelthorne didn't waste resources on aesthetics. Every building served a purpose. Every corridor was designed for function, not beauty. The people moved with their heads down, quick and purposeful, and none of them looked at me twice. In Vaelthorne, you learned not to notice things. It was a survival skill.

I dismounted at the stable block near the Inner Sanctum and handed the mare to a groom without a word. She was shaking from the ride. I should have felt something about that. I didn't. Or rather, I did feel something, and I hated it -- a small, sharp needle of guilt that had no business being there.

The Inner Sanctum was Lord Vaelthorne's seat of power. It wasn't a palace. It was a fortress within a fortress, built from the same black stone but reinforced with rift-iron that hummed faintly in the walls. The corridors were narrow by design -- easy to defend, impossible to ambush. I knew every passage, every junction, every shadowed alcove. I'd been born in these halls. Raised in them. Shaped by them.

The guards at the inner door didn't speak. They recognized me and stepped aside. One of them -- young, barely older than a boy -- had sweat on his upper lip. New. Probably terrified. I almost told him he was doing fine. The thought surprised me so much I kept walking.

Lord Vaelthorne's chamber was at the top of the eastern tower, accessible only by a single stairway that spiraled upward inside the stone like the throat of some great beast. I climbed it without hurrying. My side throbbed. The wound had reopened during the ride, and I could feel warmth spreading beneath the wolfroot packing. I'd need to see a healer eventually. Not yet. Not until I'd delivered my report.

The door was iron-bound oak, thick enough to stop a battering ram. It opened before I reached for it.

Lord Vaelthorne stood at the far window, silhouetted against the grey sky. He was tall -- taller than me, taller than most -- and he held himself with the stillness of a man who had never needed to rush for anything. His hair was silver-white, worn long, and his armor was the deep black of rift-iron, unadorned except for the single spiral sigil on his breastplate. He didn't turn when I entered.

"You're wounded," he said.

"Superficial."

"Liar." He turned. His face was angular, aristocratic, marked by decades of hard decisions. His eyes were the pale grey of winter storm clouds, and they missed nothing. "You're bleeding through your wrappings. Sit."

"I'll stand."

"You'll sit, Shadowfang. I need you functional, not stoic."

I sat. The chair was simple, wooden, deliberately uncomfortable. Vaelthorne believed comfort bred complacency. I lowered myself into it and felt the wound pull. The Voice stirred at the back of my mind -- not words, just a pulse of attention, like something opening one eye.

Vaelthorne studied me for a long moment. Then he poured two glasses of something amber from a decanter on his desk. He handed me one. I took it. The liquor burned going down, and the numbed edges of my consciousness sharpened.

"Tell me," he said.

"Aethoria knows."

He didn't react. "Elaborate."

"The Hero has awakened. The light -- it's real. Not the diluted stuff the old texts describe. This was something else. Something that makes every defense we've planned look like parchment against a wildfire." I paused, remembering. The throne room. The way the light had poured from the girl like she was cracking open from the inside. The way it had hit me -- not like heat, not like force, but like recognition. Like something in me had been seen and named.

"The Dawn Court?"

"Will not intervene. I made sure of it." I took another drink. The liquor was Vaelthorne's private stock -- aged in rift-wood casks, and it tasted like smoke and old stone. "Their ambassador received the message. He understood."

"Understood that he would die if he didn't comply?"

"Understood the strategic reality."

Vaelthorne almost smiled. It was the closest he ever came -- a slight relaxation of the muscles around his eyes, a fractional softening of the mouth. "The Dawn Court will not intervene. Aethoria is isolated." He set his glass down. "That is precisely what we needed."
