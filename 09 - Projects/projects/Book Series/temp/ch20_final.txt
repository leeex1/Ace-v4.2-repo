## Chapter 20: The Spire Assault

The war began at midnight.

I stood on the ridge overlooking Aethoria's eastern wall, the Shadow Vanguard spread behind me in a formation I had designed myself. Two hundred fifty assassins in the primary assault group, one hundred fifty in the reserve, and one hundred in the support element -- archers, engineers, and the specialists who would breach the wall. The city lay below us, its crystalline spires catching the moonlight like a field of frozen lightning, its golden walls glowing with the soft luminescence of the ancient wards that had protected it for centuries.

Those wards were about to fail.

"Status," I said.

The Vanguard's second-in-command, a lean woman named Ashara, materialized at my shoulder. She was Kaelen's choice, not mine -- a veteran of a dozen operations, quick and precise and utterly loyal to the chain of command.

"Wall breach team in position," she said. "Secondary teams are staged at the north and south approaches. Reserve is held at the fallback point. We are ready."

"The lords' orders?"

"Primary objective: the Spire of Governance. Secondary objective: the garrison at the central square. Tertiary objective... Collateral targets in the Warrens."

The Warrens. Aethoria's residential district for the working poor. The lords of the Shadow Mirror had designated them as collateral targets because their destruction would demoralize the city.

"Scratch the Warrens," I said.

Ashara's expression did not change. "Sir?"

"We target the Spire and the garrison. Nothing else."

"The lords specifically ordered --"

"I know what the lords ordered. The collateral targets in the Warrens are a waste of resources. We hit the Spire, we hit the garrison, we decapitate Aethoria's leadership. The rest falls on its own." I looked at her. "Collateral damage is wasted data. We target the Spire, not the Warrens."

A silence. Then Ashara nodded. "Understood, sir."

She disappeared into the dark. I stood on the ridge and looked at the city where my brother lived, where the Hero blazed with a light that could crack the world open, where the Unity Market stood with its shard buried beneath the old oak tree, broadcasting the signal that would call the harvest.

I was about to attack my brother's home. I was about to lead five hundred assassins through streets where children slept, where families huddled by fireplaces, where the music that Lukas loved still echoed in the walls.

And I was going to do it without killing anyone I did not have to.

The Voice was certain. The mission was clear. The purpose was absolute.

But the crack was still there, beneath the certainty, and through it, the lullaby bled like a wound that would not close.

Hush now, little shadow, the moon is keeping score.

I drew my sword. "Move out."

The wall breach went clean.

The engineers had done their work -- a section of Aethoria's eastern wall, weakened by weeks of carefully placed charges, crumbled under the Vanguard's assault like sand through a sieve. The luminescent wards flickered, sputtered, and died, and through the gap poured two hundred and fifty of the deadliest assassins in the western territories.

I was the first through.

The streets of Aethoria hit me like a memory. I had walked these streets as a boy, before Oakhaven, before Vaelthorne, before the world split into before and after. The architecture was different now -- the crystalline spires, the golden stone, the gardens that bloomed even in winter -- but the bones of the city were the same. The same streets, the same angles, the same way the wind moved through the corridors like a living thing.

I pushed the memory down. Not now. Later. If there was a later.

"First and second squads, push to the central square. Secure the garrison. Non-lethal engagements only."

"Non-lethal?" Ashara's voice, tight with disbelief.

"You heard me."

"The lords --"

"The lords want results. Non-lethal results are still results. Move."

The squads moved. I led the third through the eastern district, moving fast through streets that were still sleeping. The alarm had not been raised yet -- the wall breach had been silent, a controlled collapse rather than an explosion, and the night guards were either unconscious or dead.

Dead. I had ordered non-lethal, but Kaelen's training ran deep in these people, and the first rule of infiltration was: eliminate the watchers. I had changed the rules for the Vanguard, but the Vanguard was not the only force Vaelthorne had deployed tonight. The shock troops -- the regulars, the ones who answered directly to the lords -- had come through the northern gate with fire and noise, and they were already in the streets, burning, breaking, making the kind of entrance that lords preferred.

I heard the screams before I saw the fire. The shock troops had reached the residential blocks. Not the Warrens -- I had kept the Vanguard away from those -- but the middle districts, where the city's tradespeople lived. The lords had rerouted their forces when they realized I was not hitting the Warrens. They were making up for my restraint with their own excess.

"Third squad, redirect. Two blocks north. Stop the shock troops."

"Sir, those are our allies."

"Not tonight."

I led the squad through the burning streets, moving fast, dodging debris. The shock troops were Vaelthorne regulars -- armored, armed, and operating under the assumption that everything in their path was a target. They had broken into a residential building and were dragging people into the street. A woman. Two children. An old man who could not move fast enough.

I hit the first one from behind. A palm strike to the base of the skull, precise, calculated, dropping him like a puppet with cut strings. The second turned, sword raised, and I disarmed him -- literally, driving my blade through the tendons of his wrist and sending his sword clattering to the cobblestones. He screamed. I hit him in the throat, not hard enough to kill, hard enough to make breathing painful and speech impossible.

The third and fourth went down to my squad. We were surgical. We were precise. We were the opposite of what the shock troops had been trained to be.

The woman stared at me from the doorway, clutching her children. She saw the black armor, the face wrap, the blade. She saw a Vaelthorne assassin. And she was terrified.

"Go," I said. "East gate. Now. Before more come."

She ran. The children looked back over their shoulders, the same wide eyes I had seen in the alley in Vaelthorne. The same curiosity. The same impossible question: why?

I did not have an answer. I kept moving.

The Spire of Governance rose from the center of Aethoria like a finger pointing at the gods. It was the city's seat of power -- the council chamber, the archives, the chambers of the Hero and her allies. It was built from a single piece of crystal, mined from the deep places beneath the city, and it glowed with an inner light that never dimmed.

I reached the Spire's base with a hundred Vanguard at my back. The shock troops had been neutralized -- not killed, just stopped, their armor stripped and their weapons taken. They were bound and gagged in a side street, waiting for the battle to end and the paperwork to begin.

The Spire's entrance was defended. Of course it was. Alyra, the elven regent, was no fool, and she had anticipated an assault on the Spire. Her defenders were drawn up in a semicircle around the entrance -- Royal Guard, Lukas's people, the best Aethoria had to offer. They wore silver armor that caught the moonlight, and they held their ground with the kind of quiet determination that comes from defending something worth defending.

And at their center, sword drawn, eyes blazing, was my brother.

Lukas.

He looked exactly as I remembered. Tall, broad-shouldered, with the same dark hair and the same stubborn jaw and the same eyes that burned with the particular fire of someone who believed in things. His sword was raised, his stance was perfect, and his gaze swept the darkness like a lantern searching for shadows.

"Vaelthorne!" he called. "I know you are there. I can feel you."

Of course he could. We were brothers. We had been born in the same bed, raised in the same wheat fields, trained by the same mother before the fire took her. I could feel him too -- a warmth in my chest, a pull, like a compass needle pointing home.

I stepped forward.

"Hello, Lukas."

He saw me. The recognition hit him like a physical blow -- I saw his shoulders tense, his jaw tighten, his eyes widen. For a moment, he was not the Captain of the Royal Guard but the boy who had shared a bed with me in Oakhaven, who had held my hand during the fire, who had sworn on their mother's grave that he would find me again.

Then the moment passed, and he was the Captain again.

"Fenris." His voice was flat. Controlled. "You are attacking Aethoria."

"I am attacking the Spire. The city is incidental."

"The city is my home."

"I know." I looked at him across twenty paces of moonlit cobblestone, across twenty years of separation and silence and grief. "That is why I am here."

The Vanguard spread behind me. Lukas's Guard held their ground. The air between us crackled with the tension of a hundred weapons waiting to be drawn.

"I will not let you take the Spire," Lukas said.

"I know."

"Then why are you here?"

"Because if I do not take it, someone worse will. Someone who will not stop at the Spire. Someone who will burn the whole city to get what they want." I met his gaze. "I am trying to limit the damage, Lukas. I am trying to save who I can."

"By attacking my home?"

"By controlling the attack. The shock troops are stopped. The Warrens are untouched. The residential blocks are..." I paused, thinking of the fire, the screams. "Being handled."

Lukas stared at me. I could see the calculations happening behind his eyes -- the same stubborn, thorough, painstaking calculations that had made him the best Captain of the Royal Guard Aethoria had ever had. He was trying to understand. He was trying to reconcile the brother he remembered with the assassin standing before him.

"You are different," he said.

"So are you."

"What happened in the throne room?"

The Hero's light. The crack in the Voice. The wheat fields bleeding through the certainty.

"I am tired, Lukas. I am tired of the silence. I am tired of the killing. I am tired of being a weapon." I sheathed my sword. The Vanguard tensed behind me. Ashara's hand went to her knife. I ignored her. "I am here to take the Spire. I am here to satisfy Vaelthorne's orders. And then I am going to go home."

"Home?"

"Wherever you are."

Lukas was quiet for a long time. The moon moved overhead. The fire crackled in the distance. Somewhere in the city, a child was crying.

"The moon remembers," he said softly.

My throat tightened. It was the code phrase -- the one our mother had taught us, the one we had whispered to each other during the fire, the one that meant: I see you. I remember you. You are not alone.

"The moon remembers," I said.

We stood there, two brothers on opposite sides of a war, and the silence between us was not the silence of enemies but the silence of two people trying to find their way back to each other through a darkness that had been growing for twenty years.

"I need time," I said. "Take the Spire. Hold it. When the dust settles, we will talk."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then I will take the Spire anyway. But there will be no killing tonight. Not by my hand. Not in this city."

Lukas looked at me for a long moment. Then he lowered his sword.

"Go," he said. "Take the Spire. But Fenris --"

I stopped.

"If this is a trick, I will find you. And I will kill you. Brother or not."

"It is not a trick. It is a promise." I turned back to the Vanguard. "Move. Spire entrance. Non-lethal. The Royal Guard will not resist."

Ashara looked at me like I had lost my mind. Maybe I had. Maybe this was what sanity looked like in a world that had gone mad.

The Vanguard moved forward. The Royal Guard parted. And I walked into the Spire of Governance, the crystal walls glowing around me, the light of Aethoria's heart warming my skin.

Behind me, Lukas watched.

And somewhere in the distance, the moon kept score.
