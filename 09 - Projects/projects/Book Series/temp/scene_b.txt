Then the tunnels went dark for real.

The Synth-Stalkers came not in squads but in a tide—a wave of grey, insectoid bodies that poured from a collapsed sap-vent in the valley wall, drinking the torchlight as they came. There were hundreds, maybe a thousand, their movements twitchy and arithmetic, and they climbed over one another's backs with a patience that held no anger in it. The Auditor did not send soldiers into the roots. It sent arithmetic.

"Wall!" Hestian roared, and the Royal Guard locked shields. The phalanx braced as the tide hit, plasteel ringing against Audit-hardened claws, the front rank leaning in with their shoulders while the second rank fed kinetic pikes through the gaps. It was discipline of the old school—iron and nerve and the memory of parade-grounds that no longer existed—but the tide did not care about discipline. It climbed. Stalkers scaled their own dead to reach the shield-rim, scrabbling up the wall of locked steel, and one by one the grey claws crested the edge.

And Kray was already there. The panther-hybrid struck the apex of the shield-wall with a screech of fury, his claws raking a climber's face from its skull, his tail lashing for balance as he ran the ridge of the shields like a living fang. Two stalkers lunged from above; he kicked one down into the mass and locked the other in his jaws, shaking it like a rat, grey fluid sheeting across his muzzle.

"Your flank! Your flank!" A young Guard bellowed over the din—a boy with a helmet too big for his head and a heron sigil still fresh on his breastplate. He had thrown his pike away reaching for the panther-hybrid, and now he was sprinting along the inside of the wall, empty-handed, as a stalker raised its scythe-arm over Kray's turned back.

Kray pivoted at the last instant, planted his feet, and took the blow across his armored shoulder with a grunt of pure pain. He did not fall. He caught the stalker's wrist in both hands, bit through the joint, spat the grey, and when the creature recoiled he hurled it backward over the wall into the tide.

"You're dead, heron," he rasped, blood and static on his fangs. "You threw away your pike for a stray cat."

"You're the Wolfheart's cat," the boy shot back, red-faced and shaking, "and I wasn't about to—" The words died as a scythe-arm came spinning over the shields, and Kray slammed him flat with his body, the blade whirring past where the boy's head had been. The panther-hybrid rose, grabbed the boy's collar, and hurled him behind Hestian's veterans.

"Next time, keep the pike, heron," Kray snarled, his amber eyes burning. "I'm not worth dying for."

But Hestian, watching from the rear with his sword low and steady, saw the truth of it. The Vanguard fought like they had nothing to lose because for a decade the Coalition had given them nothing. They could not hold a line because no line had ever held for them. Yet when the wall needed teeth, they were the teeth. He reached down and hauled the boy upright—his own nephew, Fael, who had lied about his age to enlist and whose helmet he had promised to hammer down himself if the boy died beneath it.

"Fall in, Fael," Hestian said quietly. "And when the furred ones leap, you stand level and you hold. They will catch what falls over." He looked past the boy at Kray, and his voice carried. "The cat is worth dying for, nephew. We are all worth dying for. That is the only thing the Auditor cannot finalize—a line that holds because the people in it love each other."

Kray met the general's eyes for one long, grey second. Then the panther-hybrid gave the smallest possible nod—a gesture worth more than any salute, a truce older than any treaty—and turned back to the tide, claws out, singing the Vanguard's ragged hunting-cry into the dark.
