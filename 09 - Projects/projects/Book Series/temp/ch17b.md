I waited. There was more. There was always more with Vaelthorne. He dealt in layers, and the surface was always the least interesting part.

"The Hero's power," he said slowly, "is the key to the Ascension. You know this."

"I know what I've been told."

"What you've been told is accurate, for once. The Shadow Mirror has been studying the phenomenon for generations. The Hero's light is not merely a weapon -- it is a frequency. A signal. And signals can be... redirected."

The word sat in the air between us like a live coal.

"Redirected," I repeated.

"The Ascension requires an enormous source of energy. The rift-wells provide power, but not the kind we need. The rift gives and takes. It's parasitic by nature. The Hero's light, however, is generative. It creates rather than consumes. If we could channel that energy..." He let the sentence trail off. He didn't need to finish it. He'd been planning this for longer than I'd been alive.

"You want me to capture the Hero."

"I want you to do what you do best. Identify the target. Map the defenses. Find the weakness." He picked up his glass again. "And when the time comes, you will deliver her to us. Willingly or otherwise."

The Voice pulsed again. Stronger this time. Certainty. The familiar, comforting certainty that had guided every mission, every kill, every moment of my life since Vaelthorne had found me in the ruins of Oakhaven and made me into something useful.

But underneath the certainty, something else. A flicker. The face in the wheat fields.

"Understood," I said.

Vaelthorne studied me again. Those storm-grey eyes, reading me the way he read everything -- like a map, like a problem to be solved.

"There is another matter," he said. "Kaelen has requested your presence at the training grounds."

"Kaelen doesn't request."

"No. But this time, he asked. Specifically for you." Vaelthorne moved back to his desk. "The Shadow Vanguard is operational. Five hundred of our finest. Kaelen has been grooming them. But he believes they need a field commander. Someone who has seen real combat against real targets."

"He wants me to lead them."

"He doesn't want you to. He needs you to. There's a difference, and Kaelen is too proud to admit it." Vaelthorne placed both hands flat on the desk's surface. "You'll report to him at dawn. You'll take command of the Vanguard. And you'll prepare them for what's coming."

"What's coming?"

"The war, Shadowfang. The real one. Not these surgical strikes and whispered assassinations we've been waging for years. An actual war, with armies and battle lines and consequences." He looked up at me. "Aethoria believes they are safe behind their walls and their Hero. We will show them how wrong they are."

The Voice was certain now. Loud. The crack had sealed -- or rather, something had sealed it from the other side, pressing down like a hand over a wound. The Auditor's frequency filled my skull with cold precision. The mission was clear. The purpose was absolute.

And yet.

And yet I saw wheat fields. I saw my brother's face. I heard the music that Lukas used to hum when we were children, some old lullaby our mother sang before the fire took her and the world split into before and after.

"Is there anything else?" Vaelthorne asked.

"No."

"Then go. See a healer first. I need you at full strength."

I stood. The wound in my side screamed. I kept my face still.

"Shadowfang."

I stopped at the door.

"The Hero -- the girl. What is she?"

I thought about it. Not the tactical assessment. Not the power reading. The truth, raw and unprocessed, the thing I hadn't said to the Voice or to Vaelthorne or to anyone.

"She's brave," I said. "She's terrifyingly, stupidly brave. And she looked at me like she could see who I was before all this." I paused. "Like she was sorry for me."

Vaelthorne was quiet for a moment. Then: "Sentiment is a weapon, Shadowfang. Yours, apparently, is being turned against you. Be careful it doesn't cut you to the bone."

I left.

The healer's chambers were in the lower levels, where the stone was older and the air tasted like minerals and antiseptic. The healer was a woman named Thess -- small, efficient, grey-haired, with hands that never trembled and eyes that never judged. She'd patched me up more times than I could count, and she did it now without comment, unwrapping the blood-soaked bandages, cleaning the wound with something that made my vision whiten at the edges, and stitching it closed with practiced precision.

"Horse kicked you?" she asked, which was her way of asking what happened.

"Something like that."

"You should rest."

"I should do a lot of things."

She finished and rebandaged the wound. "Three days. No strenuous activity. No climbing. No fighting."

"I'll try."

"You won't try. You'll ignore everything I've said and do whatever you were going to do anyway. But I've said it, and now it's documented, so when you collapse, it won't be my fault."

I almost smiled. "Thank you, Thess."

"Don't thank me. Just don't die. I'm tired of filling out the paperwork."

I left her chambers and made my way through the lower corridors toward my quarters. Vaelthorne's inner circle lived in the Sanctum itself -- a privilege and a surveillance measure, since every room was built with the understanding that someone was always listening. My quarters were small, sparse, and exactly as I'd left them. A bed, a desk, a wardrobe, a weapons rack. No personal effects. Nothing that said: a human lives here.

I sat on the bed and pressed my palm against the wound. It was tight, the stitches holding. Thess was good at her job.

The Voice spoke.

It didn't speak in words, exactly. It never did. It was more like a knowing -- a certainty planted directly in my consciousness, bypassing language entirely. The Auditor didn't deal in words. It dealt in truths. And the truth it planted now was this:

The mission is clear. The purpose is absolute. You are the instrument. The instrument does not question the hand that wields it.

I believed it. I'd always believed it. The Voice was certainty itself -- not faith, not hope, but the cold, mathematical precision of a truth that could be proven, measured, replicated. The Auditor had spoken to me since I was twelve years old, since the night Vaelthorne's agents had found me in the rubble of Oakhaven, half-dead, screaming for a brother who was already gone. The Voice had quieted me. Had given me purpose. Had turned my grief into a blade.

But tonight, the certainty had a seam. A hairline fracture running through it like a crack in ice, and underneath the crack, something warm and dangerous was bleeding through.

Lukas's face. The wheat fields. The music.

The lullaby.

Hush now, little shadow, the moon's keeping score.
Close your eyes, little brother, I'll find you once more.

I hadn't heard that song in twenty years. I'd buried it so deep I'd forgotten it existed. But the throne room in Aethoria had cracked something open in me -- the Hero's light, maybe, or Lukas's blade, or the look in my brother's eyes when he'd said my name like it still meant something.

I pressed my hand harder against the wound. The pain helped. Pain was simple. Pain was honest. Pain didn't whisper lullabies.

The door opened. I reached for a knife -- instinct, always instinct -- and then I recognized the silhouette in the doorway.

Kaelen.

He filled the frame the way he filled every space he occupied: completely, unapologetically, with the sheer gravitational weight of a man who had never once questioned his place in the world. He was massive -- six and a half feet, shoulders like a yoke, arms thick as bridge cables. His face was weathered, scarred, marked by decades of combat and command. His eyes were dark, almost black, and they held the flat, assessing gaze of a predator evaluating prey.

"Shadowfang," he said. His voice was deep, resonant, the kind of voice that didn't need volume to command attention.

"Kaelen."

He stepped inside and closed the door behind him. He didn't sit. Kaelen never sat unless he chose to -- it was a habit I'd always found theatrical, but effective.

"You're wounded," he said.

"Everyone keeps telling me."

"Because it's obvious. You move like a man carrying twice his weight." He crossed his arms. "The lords are pleased. The message was delivered. The Dawn Court is neutralized."

"Then why are you here?"

"Because I watched the reports from Aethoria. And I saw something I haven't seen in twenty years of working with you." He paused. "Hesitation."

The word landed like a slap.

"In the throne room," he continued. "When the Hero awakened. You had a clean window -- three seconds, maybe four -- to complete the secondary objective. The girl. You didn't take it."

"The light --"

"The light is irrelevant. You've operated in adverse conditions before. Rift-storms, null-fields, warded chambers. The light was not the problem." He uncrossed his arms. "The problem was that you looked at her, and something in you stopped."

I met his gaze. Kaelen was the only person in Vaelthorne I couldn't lie to -- not because he was perceptive, though he was, but because he'd trained me. He'd shaped me from a screaming child into the most effective assassin in the western territories. He knew my tells the way a sculptor knows the grain of stone.

"She reminded me of something," I said. Truth, delivered precisely.

"Of what?"

"My brother."

Kaelen was quiet for a moment. Then he nodded, once, a sharp downward motion.

"That is a problem," he said. "But it's also why I'm here." He moved to the small window -- a narrow slit in the stone, barely wide enough to see through. "The Shadow Vanguard is ready. Five hundred of them. The best I've ever trained. But they're raw. They've drilled, they've sparred, they've run simulations. They haven't bled."

"And you want me to bleed them."

"I want you to lead them. Tomorrow, at first light, you take command. You'll run them through combat scenarios -- urban combat, infiltration, target acquisition. You'll push them past their limits and then past the limits beyond that." He turned from the window. "And you'll do it because you are the only person in this city who understands what it means to fight in a world that doesn't want you in it."

"Flattery."

"Truth. The lords see the Vanguard as a tool. Vaelthorne sees them as a resource. I see them as my life's work, and I won't hand them to someone who'll treat them like disposable weapons." He fixed me with that dark stare. "I'm handing them to you because you were once like them. A weapon that learned to think. And thinking weapons are the most dangerous kind."

I said nothing. The Voice was loud in my skull -- certainty, purpose, the cold mathematics of obedience. But underneath it, quieter but persistent, was the crack. The seam. The warm, dangerous thing bleeding through from the place where Lukas's face still lived.

"At dawn," I said.

"At dawn." Kaelen moved to the door. He stopped, one hand on the frame, and for a moment -- just a moment -- something shifted in his expression. Not softness, exactly. Kaelen didn't do softness. But a flicker of something that might have been concern, if he'd had the vocabulary for it.

"The lords will be watching," he said. "Vaelthorne will be watching. The Auditor will be watching." He paused. "Don't give them a reason to doubt you."

He left.

I sat in the silence of my quarters and listened to the wound in my side pulsing with my heartbeat. The Voice was certain. The mission was clear. The purpose was absolute.

But the lullaby was louder.

Hush now, little shadow, the moon's keeping score.
Close your eyes, little brother, I'll find you once more.

I pressed my palms against my eyes until I saw stars. The stars became wheat fields. The wheat fields became fire. The fire became a throne room filled with light, and a girl -- young, terrified, blazing -- who looked at me like she could see every broken thing inside me and still chose to believe I could be unbroken.

"I'm coming home, Lukas," I whispered. "I don't know how. But I'm coming home."

The Voice said nothing. The crack held. And for the first time in twenty years, I fell asleep without the certainty.

I dreamed of wheat fields instead.
