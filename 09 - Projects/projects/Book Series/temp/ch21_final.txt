## Chapter 21: The Spy's Report

Nyssa had been inside the Vaelthorne camp for six hours before they caught her.

She had entered through the supply lines -- the weakest point in any military operation, the place where discipline frayed and attention wandered. The Vaelthorne camp was a sprawling complex of tents and temporary structures pitched in the fields east of Aethoria, a small city of black canvas and iron that housed the rear elements of the invasion force. The guards were competent but overextended. The perimeter was held by units that had been awake for thirty-six hours. The supply wagons came and went with minimal inspection.

Nyssa rode in on a supply cart, hidden beneath bales of dried grass, breathing through a reed that poked through the top of the pile. The grass smelled like dust and insects and the particular staleness of something that had been cut too long ago. She lay still, barely breathing, listening to the guards check the cart.

"Next."

"Grain and provisions. Bound for the command tent."

"Go."

The cart rolled through. Nyssa waited until it stopped, then slipped out between the bales, dropping silently into the mud between two supply tents. She was dressed in Vaelthorne colors -- black leather, face wrap, the works. The uniform was stolen from a dead man three days ago, and it fit her like it had been made for her. Lukas had taught her that. Fit was everything. A uniform that did not fit drew attention, and attention drew questions.

She moved through the camp like smoke through a lattice -- finding the gaps, flowing through them, leaving no trace. The camp was organized along Vaelthorne lines: command tent at the center, officers' quarters in a ring around it, enlisted barracks on the perimeter. The guards moved in patterns that Nyssa had memorized from the patrol schedules she had stolen two nights ago.

Her target was the command tent.

She reached it in fifteen minutes. The tent was large, ringed with guards, and lit from within by a cold blue glow that meant rift-lamps. Two figures moved inside -- large silhouettes, one massive and still, the other lean and pacing. Nyssa pressed her ear to the canvas and listened.

"-- not following the plan," the lean silhouette was saying. The voice was sharp, military, clipped. Kaelen. "He has deviated on every major engagement. The Warrens were untouched. The residential blocks were barely touched. He stopped the shock troops."

"Shadowfang has his reasons," the massive silhouette said. Deeper. Slower. A voice like stones grinding together. Nyssa did not recognize it. "The lords will want an explanation."

"The lords will want his head." Kaelen's silhouette stopped pacing. "He is compromised. Something happened in the throne room. Something the Hero did to him."

"Then we deal with it."

"Not yet. Not until the operation is complete." A pause. "I trained him. I can fix him."

"You cannot fix what is broken, Kaelen."

Silence. Then Kaelen's voice again, quieter: "He is not broken. He is evolving. And evolution is dangerous in a weapon."

Nyssa had heard enough. She pulled back from the canvas and began to map the tent's layout in her mind. Entry points. Guard rotations. The position of the command tables, the maps, the communications array. She needed to get inside, copy the operational plans, and get out before dawn.

She made it as far as the tent's rear flap.

The knife came from nowhere -- one moment she was crouched in the shadow of the tent, and the next a blade was at her throat, held with the kind of precise, practiced pressure that said: I know exactly how much force it takes to open your arteries, and I am choosing not to use it.

"Move," said a voice behind her.

Nyssa moved. She was escorted around the tent and through the front entrance, where Kaelen and the massive figure were waiting. The massive figure was a man she had never seen before -- old, scarred, with a face like a topographical map and eyes that held the flat, assessing gaze of a professional killer. He wore Vaelthorne's black, but his armor was different -- heavier, more ornate, etched with symbols Nyssa did not recognize.

Kaelen looked at her with something that might have been respect.

"Good," he said. "Very good. Six hours inside the perimeter without triggering a single alarm. Lukas trained you well."

Nyssa said nothing. The knife at her throat was still there, held by a guard who had appeared from the shadows.

"Search her."

They searched her. They found the lockpicks sewn into her boot heel, the tiny blade hidden in her belt buckle, the information cache tucked into the lining of her coat. They found everything.

Except the real message. The one she had already sent. The silver arrow, fired into the sky three hours ago, arcing over the camp toward Aethoria, carrying a scroll of parchment that contained everything she had learned about the Vaelthorne operation.

The guard finished the search and stepped back. Kaelen studied Nyssa with those dark, unreadable eyes.

"Who sent you?"

"You know who sent me."

"Lukas. Of course." Kaelen crossed his arms. "The Captain of the Royal Guard, sending his spy into our camp. How quaint. How nostalgic." He tilted his head. "What did you learn?"

"Enough."

"Enough to what? Report back? Tell Lukas that Shadowfang has gone soft? That his brother has developed a conscience?" Kaelen almost smiled. "We already know that. We are trying to decide what to do about it."

The massive figure spoke. "Interrogate her. Find out what she has sent back to Aethoria. If the Royal Guard knows our operational plans --"

"She has already sent it," Nyssa said.

Both men looked at her.

"The silver arrow," she said. "Three hours ago. Directed at the Spire. Your plans, your force composition, your command structure. Everything." She met Kaelen's gaze. "You should assume I know everything."

A silence. Then Kaelen laughed. It was a short, sharp sound, like a blade striking stone.

"Well," he said. "At least she is competent." He turned to the massive figure. "We will need to accelerate the timeline. If Aethoria knows our plans --"

"She is my prisoner," the massive figure said. "I will interrogate her myself."

"She is no one's prisoner yet." Kaelen turned back to Nyssa. "She is a messenger. And messages can be sent in both directions."

He leaned close. His breath smelled like iron and old leather.

"What would you say to Shadowfang, if you could speak to him?"

"I would say he is a traitor to both sides."

Kaelen's eyes narrowed. "Would you now."

"I would say he is playing both ends against the middle, and when the dust settles, he will have nothing. No home, no allies, no purpose. Just a brother who will never fully trust him and a city that will never fully accept him."

"And what would you say to Lukas?"

Nyssa was quiet for a moment. Then: "I would say his brother is trying to come home. And I would say that is the most dangerous thing in this entire war."

The interrogation room was a storage tent near the camp's perimeter, stripped of everything except a chair, a table, and a lantern that cast more shadow than light. Nyssa sat in the chair, hands bound behind her back, and waited.

The guard left. The tent was silent except for the distant sounds of the camp. Nyssa listened to it all with the particular attention of someone who had learned to extract information from the texture of sound.

She heard footsteps approaching. Not Kaelen. Not the massive figure. Lighter. Faster. The particular rhythm of someone who moved like they were born in shadows.

The tent flap opened.

Shadowfang.

He looked exactly as she had expected. Lean, angular, dressed in the black of Vaelthorne, with a face that was more scar tissue than skin and eyes that held the flat, dead gaze of a man who had killed more people than he could count. But there was something else in those eyes tonight. Something she had never seen before.

Uncertainty.

"They caught you," he said.

"Your people are getting better."

"My people did not catch you. Kaelen did." He moved to the table and sat. "What did you send to Aethoria?"

"Everything."

He nodded. "The operational plans. Force composition. Command structure."

"All of it."

"And the thing about me?"

Nyssa studied him. In the lantern light, his face was a landscape of shadows -- deep lines, sharp angles, the kind of permanent tension that comes from years of holding yourself together by force of will.

"I told Lukas you were developing a conscience," she said.

"Did you."

"I told him you were rerouting patrols away from civilian areas. Stopping the shock troops. Taking the Spire without killing anyone." She leaned forward. "I told him you were trying to come home."

Shadowfang was quiet for a long time. The lantern flickered. The camp murmured.

"Tell me something, Nyssa," he said. "When you watch me -- when you see the things I do -- what do you see?"

"A man who is lost."

"Lost."

"You were a weapon. You were the perfect weapon. And now you are not, and you do not know what you are instead."

He almost smiled. It was a terrible thing to see -- a smile on a face that had forgotten how to do it, a flicker of humanity in eyes that had been trained to be empty.

"Tell Lukas I am coming home," he said. "But I need time."

"Time for what?"

"To finish this. To make it right. To..." He stopped. The uncertainty in his eyes deepened. "To find out if there is anything left of me that is worth finding."

Nyssa studied him. She did not trust him. She would not trust him, not ever, not completely. He was Vaelthorne's weapon, the Shadowfang, the most dangerous assassin in the western territories. He had killed more people than she had years.

But she saw something in his eyes that she recognized. Something she had seen in Lukas's eyes, in the quiet hours before dawn, when the Captain of the Royal Guard sat alone in his office and stared at the walls and wondered if any of it mattered.

The particular loneliness of someone who had been asked to do impossible things and was beginning to realize the cost.

"You are a traitor to both sides," she said.

"I am a bridge," he said. "Just like him."

Nyssa considered this. A bridge. Between Vaelthorne and Aethoria. Between the old world and the new. Between the weapon he had been and the man he was trying to become.

Bridges got burned on both sides.

"I will tell him," she said. "But I will not promise anything."

"That is all I ask."

He stood and moved to the tent flap. He stopped, one hand on the canvas, and spoke without turning around.

"The guard outside will have a moment of inattention in about thirty seconds. He will look away toward the supply tents. When he does, go."

"And if I do not?"

"Then Kaelen will find you, and I will not be able to stop what happens next."

He left. Nyssa sat in the chair and counted to thirty.

At twenty-seven, she heard the guard's footsteps shift. At twenty-eight, she heard him turn. At twenty-nine, she slipped her bonds -- she had been working them since she sat down, rubbing the rope against the sharp edge of the chair, weakening the fibers -- and at thirty, she was out of the tent, silent as smoke, moving through the camp like a shadow within shadows.

She made it to the perimeter in four minutes. She made it through the perimeter in two. She made it to the tree line in one.

Behind her, the Vaelthorne camp hummed with the particular energy of an army preparing for war. Ahead of her, the dark forest waited, patient and indifferent, full of paths that led to places where a spy could rest, recover, and send another message.

She pulled the last silver arrow from her quiver, loaded it, and fired. The arrow arced into the sky, catching the moonlight, disappearing into the dark.

To: Lukas, Captain of the Royal Guard
From: N
Subject: Shadowfang

He is coming home. He says he needs time. I do not know if he means it. I do not know if it matters.

But I saw his eyes, Lukas. And for the first time since I started watching him, I saw something in them that was not empty.

Be careful. He is more dangerous now than he ever was as a weapon. Because weapons do not hope. And hopeful men do stupid, brave, impossible things.

The arrow vanished into the dark.

Nyssa pulled her cloak tighter and disappeared into the trees.
